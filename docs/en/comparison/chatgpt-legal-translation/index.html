Corrext and generative AI. Useful for understanding, but binding documents need a framework

ChatGPT, Azure OpenAI GPT and Claude rephrase, summarise and explain a legal passage better than a conventional translation engine. This page sets out what they do well, and what they cannot prove on their own on a binding Swiss document.

Request a demo

The short answer

Yes, a general-purpose LLM translates and rephrases a legal passage very well.

What it does not provide on its own: sourced Swiss terminology, a guaranteed legal reference, a review interface, a quote, and data processing in Switzerland. In Corrext, Azure OpenAI GPT and Claude remain available, under your organisation's governance.

  • No sourced Swiss terminology

    A term is rendered plausibly, with nothing to show how the Federal Gazette or fedlex.admin.ch actually renders it. In Corrext, that is the job of Fast lookup CHnell.

  • No guarantee on legal references

    An article, an abbreviation or a court can be produced with full confidence and still be wrong, with no warning sign in the answer.

  • No review interface

    No validation chain: no review level, no legal translator to order, no certification possible. In Corrext, review is ordered in Translation projects.

What general-purpose LLMs do remarkably well

They are the best tools legal experts have ever had for working on the meaning of a text. Saying so does not weaken our argument: it is the honest starting point for the comparison.

  • Rephrasing and simplifying

    Turning a dense clause into a readable paragraph for a client who is not a legal expert, or the reverse, in the register requested.

  • Summarising and explaining

    Bringing out the structure of a 50-page written submission, explaining a contractual mechanism, isolating each party's obligations.

  • Handling ambiguity

    Offering several readings of an ambiguous wording and spelling out what separates them, where a conventional translation engine silently picks one.

  • Exploring drafts

    Quickly producing several versions of a clause to feed an internal discussion, before settling on the version that will be negotiated.

Twelve criteria, based on real work on a case file

What we say about general-purpose LLMs is limited to what is public and stable. They are generic models, remarkable with language. They have no sourced corpus of Swiss law and no integrated review or certification chain, and their consumer plans process documents outside Switzerland. In Corrext, Azure OpenAI GPT and Claude are configured as third-party engines hosted abroad.

Frequently asked questions about general-purpose LLMs and Swiss law

Can I use ChatGPT to translate a contract?

To understand a clause, grasp its scope or prepare a discussion, yes: a general-purpose LLM does this very well. For a contract that will be signed, sent to a third party or filed with an authority, no, not on its own. It provides no sourced Swiss terminology, no guarantee on legal references, no review by a legal translator and no data processing in Switzerland. In Corrext, these models remain available, but the binding version goes through Light, Full or Double review.

How do I check a Swiss legal reference?

Do not ask the engine that has just produced it: ask a source. In Corrext, highlight the term or abbreviation in the source text and click Fast Lookup. The CHnell concordancer displays aligned segments from official Swiss sources, with the domain, the source and a Show in context link. You then see how the term is actually rendered in the Federal Gazette or on fedlex.admin.ch, not how a model assumes it should be.

Does Corrext use general-purpose LLMs?

Yes. Azure OpenAI GPT and Claude are available in Corrext, alongside LexMachina and DeepL Pro, and you choose the engine each time you send a text. Because they are hosted abroad, selecting them triggers the Attorney-Client privilege notice, which restates your organisation's policy: full pseudonymisation, no sensitive matters. The Alternatives panel lets you compare their suggestions with the Swiss engine's on the same sentence.

Do general-purpose LLMs make up statutory provisions?

It can produce a reference, a court or a term that is plausible but wrong, and nothing in its answer flags the error. In the banking law sentence shown above, ChatGPT keeps the French abbreviation LB where the Italian versions of federal texts use LBCR. That is precisely why Corrext displays several alternatives, each labelled with its engine, and gives access to the concordancer.

Are my conversations with a consumer AI assistant confidential?

Terms vary from one provider and one plan to another, and they change. Two points remain constant for a Swiss law firm: the document is transferred outside Switzerland, and nothing in the interface reminds users what their internal policy requires before they send it. In Corrext, the Attorney-Client privilege notice appears when the engine is chosen, and Highly sensitive content mode requires storage and processing exclusively in Switzerland.

When is a general-purpose LLM the right tool?

When you are working on meaning rather than on the final wording: summarising a long document, explaining a contractual mechanism, rephrasing a paragraph for a client without legal training, exploring several drafts of a clause. Corrext also offers its own rewriting function, Rephrasing, in five styles including Formal legal, Financial and Simplified.
